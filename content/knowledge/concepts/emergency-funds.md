# Emergency Funds and Cash Management

An emergency fund is a dedicated pool of liquid savings set aside for unexpected expenses or income disruption. It is the foundation of financial resilience and a prerequisite before aggressive investing.

## Why You Need an Emergency Fund

Without one, any unexpected expense — job loss, medical bill, car breakdown, appliance failure — forces you to:
- Draw down investments at potentially the worst time (markets often fall during economic downturns, coinciding with job losses)
- Take on high-interest debt (credit cards, personal loans)
- Sell assets with CGT implications

An emergency fund prevents financial shocks from cascading into long-term wealth destruction.

## How Much to Hold

The standard recommendation is **3–6 months of essential living expenses**. This covers:
- Housing (rent or mortgage)
- Utilities
- Food and transport
- Insurance premiums
- Minimum debt repayments

Factors suggesting a larger fund (6–12 months):
- Self-employed or variable income (doctors doing locum work, contractors)
- Only one income in a household
- High fixed expenses or dependants
- Industry with volatile employment

Factors allowing a smaller fund (3 months):
- Two stable incomes in a household
- Very secure employment (government, established profession)
- Existing accessible equity in property or offset account

## Where to Keep It

Emergency funds must be **liquid** (accessible within days) and **safe** (no capital risk). Options:
- **High-interest savings accounts (HISAs)**: Earn competitive interest while remaining accessible
- **Mortgage offset account**: Reduces mortgage interest equivalent to earning the mortgage rate on savings — effectively a very high-risk-free return
- **Term deposits with short terms**: Acceptable if you stagger maturity dates, but less flexible
- **Cash management accounts**: Provided by brokers, reasonable interest rates

Do not keep emergency funds in:
- Shares or ETFs (can fall 30%+ exactly when you need them)
- Property (illiquid)
- Super (cannot access until preservation age except in very limited circumstances)
- Crypto (too volatile)

## Cash Flow Management

Beyond the emergency fund, good cash flow management involves:
- **Tracking income vs expenses**: Know your actual monthly cash flow
- **Separating spending and saving accounts**: Automate transfers to savings on payday
- **Cash flow smoothing for variable-income earners**: Build a buffer in good months to cover slow months
- **Sinking funds**: Setting aside small amounts monthly for known irregular expenses (car registration, insurance premiums, holidays)

## Opportunity Cost

Holding too much cash is its own risk — opportunity cost. Cash earning 4% when equities might return 8–10% long-term means foregone wealth accumulation. Once a sufficient emergency fund is established, additional cash should generally be deployed into appropriate long-term investments.
