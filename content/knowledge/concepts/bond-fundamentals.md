# Bond Fundamentals

Bonds are debt securities — when you buy a bond, you are lending money to the issuer (government or company) in exchange for regular interest payments and the return of your principal at maturity. They are the foundation of the fixed income asset class.

## Key Bond Terminology

**Face value (par)**: The amount the issuer will repay at maturity, typically $1,000 per bond.

**Coupon rate**: The annual interest rate paid on the face value. A $1,000 bond with a 5% coupon pays $50 per year (usually semi-annually: $25 every 6 months).

**Maturity date**: When the issuer repays the face value. Bonds range from short-term (under 2 years) to long-term (30 years+).

**Yield**: The effective return on a bond given its current market price. If a $1,000 bond with a $50 coupon is trading at $950, the yield is higher than 5% because you paid less than face value.

**Yield to maturity (YTM)**: The total return assuming the bond is held until maturity and all coupons are reinvested at the same rate.

## Bond Price vs Yield: The Inverse Relationship

This is the most important concept in fixed income: **bond prices and yields move in opposite directions**.

When interest rates rise:
- New bonds are issued with higher coupons (more attractive)
- Existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive
- Existing bond prices fall to offer a competitive yield

When interest rates fall:
- Existing bonds with higher coupons become more valuable
- Existing bond prices rise

This is why bonds fell significantly in value when central banks raised rates aggressively in 2022.

## Duration

Duration measures a bond's sensitivity to interest rate changes. It is expressed in years but represents the percentage change in price for a 1% change in yield.

- A bond with duration 5 will fall approximately 5% in price if yields rise 1%
- Longer maturity bonds have higher duration (more sensitive to rate changes)
- Short-duration bonds or cash-like instruments are less sensitive

## Types of Bonds

**Government bonds (sovereign bonds)**:
- Australian Commonwealth Government Securities (AGS)
- US Treasuries: T-bills (under 1 year), T-notes (2–10 years), T-bonds (20–30 years)
- UK Gilts, German Bunds
- Very low default risk for investment-grade governments

**Corporate bonds**: Issued by companies. Investment grade (BBB– and above) or high yield (below BBB–). Higher yield to compensate for credit risk.

**Inflation-linked bonds**: Coupon and principal adjust with inflation (e.g., Australian indexed bonds, US TIPS). Protect purchasing power in inflationary environments.

## Credit Risk

Credit risk is the risk that the issuer cannot repay. Credit rating agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) assess issuers:
- AAA, AA, A, BBB: Investment grade
- BB, B, CCC, CC, C: Speculative/junk
- D: Default

Credit spreads — the yield premium corporate bonds pay over equivalent government bonds — widen during recessions as default risk rises.

## Bonds in a Portfolio

Bonds traditionally provide stability and income. They have historically been negatively correlated with equities — when shares fall (recession fears), government bond prices often rise (investors seek safety, central banks cut rates). This makes bonds a useful portfolio diversifier, though this correlation broke down in 2022 when both fell simultaneously due to inflation driving rate rises.
