# Portfolio Theory and Diversification

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), developed by Harry Markowitz in 1952, provides the mathematical framework for how combining assets with different return and risk characteristics can produce better risk-adjusted outcomes than holding individual assets.

## The Core Idea: Diversification

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket" has real mathematical backing. When you combine assets whose returns do not move in perfect lockstep, the portfolio's total volatility is lower than the weighted average of the individual assets' volatilities.

**Correlation** is the key metric — it ranges from -1 (perfectly opposite) to +1 (perfectly aligned):
- Correlation of +1: no diversification benefit
- Correlation of 0: significant diversification benefit
- Correlation of -1: maximum diversification benefit (assets offset each other perfectly)

## Efficient Frontier

MPT shows there is an "efficient frontier" of portfolios that offer the maximum expected return for each level of risk. Portfolios below the frontier are suboptimal — you can get more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return, by adjusting your allocation.

## Systematic vs Unsystematic Risk

**Unsystematic risk (company-specific)**: The risk that a particular company performs poorly — management failures, accounting fraud, product recalls. This risk is diversifiable — it can be largely eliminated by holding enough different assets.

**Systematic risk (market risk)**: The risk of broad market movements (recessions, interest rate changes, geopolitical events). This cannot be diversified away — it affects all assets to varying degrees.

Holding 20–30 uncorrelated stocks eliminates most unsystematic risk. Holding 500 stocks (like the S&P 500) eliminates it almost entirely.

## Beta

Beta measures an asset's sensitivity to market movements. A beta of 1.0 means the asset moves with the market. A beta of 1.5 means it amplifies market moves by 50%. A beta of 0.5 is less volatile than the market. High-beta stocks (growth, tech) are more volatile; low-beta stocks (utilities, consumer staples) are more stable.

## The 60/40 Portfolio

The classic 60% equities / 40% bonds portfolio has been a benchmark for balanced investing for decades. The logic: bonds typically rise when equities fall (negative correlation in normal conditions), providing a cushion during share market drawdowns. However, in 2022 both fell simultaneously as inflation drove rates up sharply, highlighting that this correlation is not always reliable.

## Factor Investing

Research shows that certain "factors" have historically delivered above-market returns over the long run:
- **Value**: Cheap stocks (low P/E, low P/B) tend to outperform growth stocks over time
- **Size**: Small-cap stocks have historically outperformed large-caps (with higher volatility)
- **Momentum**: Stocks that have recently risen tend to continue rising in the short term
- **Quality**: Companies with strong profitability and low debt tend to outperform
- **Low volatility**: Lower-volatility stocks have surprisingly strong risk-adjusted returns

Factor ETFs are available on the ASX (e.g., QUAL for quality, VLUE for value).

## Rebalancing

Over time, faster-growing assets (equities in a bull market) become a larger share of the portfolio than intended. Rebalancing — periodically selling some of the outperformers and buying underperformers — restores the intended allocation and enforces a "buy low, sell high" discipline.
