# ETFs vs Managed Funds

Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) and managed funds are both pooled investment vehicles, but they differ in structure, cost, and access. Understanding the differences helps you choose the right vehicle for your situation.

## What is a Managed Fund?

A managed fund (also called a unit trust or mutual fund) pools money from many investors and is managed by a professional fund manager. Investors buy units at the fund's net asset value (NAV), calculated once per day after markets close. You apply through the fund manager or a platform like Netwealth, HUB24, or BT Panorama.

## What is an ETF?

An ETF is a managed fund that is listed on a stock exchange and traded like a share throughout the trading day. ETFs can be passive (tracking an index) or active (managed by a fund manager), but most popular ETFs are passive.

## Key Differences

| Feature | ETF | Managed Fund |
|---|---|---|
| Trading | Real-time on exchange | Once daily at NAV |
| Minimum investment | One unit (~$50–$200) | Often $1,000–$5,000 |
| Brokerage cost | Yes (per trade) | No (but may have entry/exit fees) |
| Management fee | 0.04%–0.60% (passive) | 0.50%–2.0%+ (active) |
| Tax efficiency | High (low internal turnover) | Lower (redemptions trigger CGT) |
| Transparency | Daily holdings disclosure | Monthly or quarterly |
| Access | Any broker account | Platform or direct |

## Passive vs Active Management

**Passive (index) investing**: The fund tracks an index (e.g., S&P 500, ASX 200). No stock selection — just replicate the index. Very low fees, highly tax-efficient, broad diversification. Evidence shows most active managers underperform their benchmark index after fees over the long run.

**Active management**: A team of analysts selects stocks they believe will outperform. Higher fees justified (in theory) by superior returns. Some active managers do outperform over extended periods, but identifying them in advance is difficult.

## The Evidence on Active Management

Multiple long-term studies (SPIVA, Morningstar Active/Passive Barometer) consistently show that the majority of active managers underperform their benchmark index after fees over 1, 5, 10, and 15-year periods. The margin widens over time due to fee compounding. However, genuine skill exists in certain managers, particularly in less efficient markets (small-cap, emerging markets).

## When Managed Funds May Be Preferable

- Access to specialist strategies not available in ETF form (e.g., certain alternative strategies, niche private market exposures)
- When using an adviser's platform that provides research, rebalancing, and tax reporting
- Regular savings plans with no brokerage cost
- Some unlisted property trusts or infrastructure funds

## When ETFs Are Preferable

- Self-directed investing without an adviser
- Minimising fees and maximising tax efficiency
- Access via a standard brokerage account
- When the investment strategy (index tracking) is well-served by passive products

## Direct Indexing

An emerging option for high-net-worth investors: owning the individual shares that make up an index, rather than the fund. Allows precise tax-loss harvesting and customisation (e.g., excluding specific stocks). Not yet widely accessible for typical Australian retail investors but growing through platforms like Sharesight and parametric fund managers.
