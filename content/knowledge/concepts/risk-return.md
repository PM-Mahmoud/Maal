# Risk and Return

Every investment involves a trade-off between risk and return. Understanding this trade-off is fundamental to building a portfolio aligned with your goals and temperament.

## What is Investment Risk?

Risk in investing is not simply the chance of losing money — it is more precisely **uncertainty about returns**. This is typically measured as volatility (standard deviation of returns).

**Volatility**: How much returns fluctuate around their average. A share that returns +30%, −15%, +20%, −5%, +25% over five years has the same average return as one that returns +11% each year, but is far more risky to hold because of the ups and downs.

**Maximum drawdown**: The peak-to-trough decline in value. The S&P 500 fell 57% from peak to trough in the 2008–09 financial crisis. An investor who sold at the bottom locked in that loss permanently.

**Sequence of returns risk**: Particularly relevant in retirement — experiencing large losses early in drawdown phase permanently reduces the portfolio's ability to recover, even if long-run average returns are fine.

## The Risk-Return Spectrum

From lowest to highest expected return (and risk):
1. Cash and deposits
2. Government bonds (short-term)
3. Government bonds (long-term)
4. Corporate bonds (investment grade)
5. Corporate bonds (high yield)
6. Property / REITs
7. Global large-cap equities
8. Australian equities
9. Small-cap equities
10. Emerging market equities
11. Venture capital / private equity

## Risk Tolerance vs Risk Capacity

**Risk tolerance**: Your psychological ability to withstand portfolio losses without panic selling. Behavioural — how you feel when markets fall 30%.

**Risk capacity**: Your financial ability to absorb losses given your income, expenses, debt, and time horizon. Objective — how much loss you can actually afford.

Both must be considered. A young investor with stable income has high risk capacity, but if they cannot sleep through a 40% drawdown, their risk tolerance limits what they should hold.

## The Sharpe Ratio

The Sharpe ratio measures return per unit of risk: (Return − Risk-free rate) ÷ Standard deviation.

A higher Sharpe ratio means better risk-adjusted returns. Comparing investments using the Sharpe ratio reveals which provides more return for each unit of risk taken. Diversification improves the Sharpe ratio of a portfolio.

## Time Horizon and Risk

Risk and time horizon are deeply connected. Over short periods (1–2 years), share markets can be highly unpredictable. Over 20+ years, the probability of equities outperforming cash and bonds approaches near-certainty historically. This is why long-term investors can afford to hold more growth assets.

## Behavioural Risk

The biggest risk many investors face is themselves — panic selling during downturns, chasing recent top performers, or abandoning a strategy after short-term losses. Studies consistently show that investor returns lag fund returns due to poor timing of entries and exits.

Dollar-cost averaging (regular, fixed contributions regardless of market level) is one tool that reduces behavioural risk by removing the temptation to time markets.
