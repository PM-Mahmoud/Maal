# Islamic Finance Principles

Islamic finance operates according to Sharia (Islamic law) principles that prohibit certain transactions while encouraging ethical, equitable financial relationships. The global Islamic finance industry manages over $3 trillion in assets.

## Core Prohibitions

**Riba (interest/usury)**: The prohibition of interest is the defining feature of Islamic finance. Charging or paying a predetermined return on money lent is not permitted, regardless of whether it is called interest or something else. This affects conventional loans, bonds, mortgages, savings accounts, and any fixed-return debt instrument.

**Gharar (excessive uncertainty)**: Contracts with excessive ambiguity, deception, or speculation are prohibited. Conventional insurance and some derivatives fall under scrutiny here (though scholars differ on modern insurance when operated as cooperative takaful).

**Maysir (gambling)**: Speculation with no underlying economic activity, relying purely on chance, is prohibited. This creates challenges for certain derivatives and short-selling strategies.

**Haram industries**: Businesses with primary operations in alcohol, pork, conventional financial services, weapons manufacturing, tobacco, pornography, and entertainment that conflicts with Islamic values are excluded.

## Key Islamic Finance Structures

**Murabaha (cost-plus financing)**: The financier buys the asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed markup. Used in place of conventional loans for property, vehicles, and equipment. The customer knows the total cost upfront and pays in instalments.

**Ijara (leasing)**: The financier buys the asset and leases it to the customer, who may eventually take ownership. Used for home finance and equipment leasing. The return is rental rather than interest.

**Musharaka (partnership)**: Both parties contribute capital and share profits and losses according to agreed ratios. Used in business finance and some property structures. Diminishing musharaka is used for home finance — the financier's share gradually reduces as the customer buys them out.

**Mudaraba (profit-sharing)**: One party provides capital; the other provides labour/expertise. Profits are shared; losses fall on the capital provider. Used in investment fund structures.

**Sukuk (Islamic bonds)**: Asset-backed securities that provide returns through asset ownership, rent, or partnership rather than interest. The global sukuk market has grown significantly, with issuances from governments (Malaysia, UAE, UK, Australia) and corporations.

## Zakat

Zakat is the obligatory charitable contribution for Muslims with wealth above the nisab threshold. It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam. Zakat on savings and investments is typically 2.5% of the value held for one lunar year above the nisab threshold (approximately the value of 85 grams of gold, roughly AUD $8,000–$12,000 depending on gold prices).

## Takaful (Islamic Insurance)

Conventional insurance involves elements of gharar and riba. Takaful is a cooperative mutual guarantee scheme — participants contribute to a pool, and claims are paid from the pool. Surplus returns to participants. Islamic scholars generally permit takaful while raising issues with conventional insurance.

## Islamic Finance in Australia

Australia has a small but growing Islamic finance sector:
- **Hejaz Financial Services**: Home finance and super fund
- **MCCA (Muslim Community Co-operative Australia)**: Home finance and savings
- **Islamic Finance Australia (IFA)**: Property and personal finance
- **Crescent Wealth**: APRA-regulated Islamic super fund

The Australian government has explored legislative changes to facilitate sukuk issuance and Islamic home finance by removing double stamp duty on Murabaha structures (which technically involves two property transfers).
