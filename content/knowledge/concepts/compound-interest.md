# Compound Interest

Compound interest is earning returns not just on your original investment but on all accumulated returns as well. Albert Einstein reportedly called it the eighth wonder of the world. Over long periods, the compounding effect is profound.

## Simple vs Compound Interest

**Simple interest**: Interest calculated only on the original principal. $10,000 at 7% simple interest earns $700 each year regardless of accumulated interest. After 30 years: $31,000.

**Compound interest**: Interest calculated on principal plus accumulated interest. $10,000 at 7% compound interest:
- Year 1: $10,700
- Year 5: $14,026
- Year 10: $19,672
- Year 20: $38,697
- Year 30: $76,123

The difference is $76,123 vs $31,000 — more than double — purely from compounding.

## The Formula

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)

Where:
- A = final amount
- P = principal (starting amount)
- r = annual interest rate (as decimal)
- n = number of times interest compounds per year
- t = time in years

For most investments, monthly compounding is used (n=12).

## The Rule of 72

A quick mental calculation: divide 72 by the annual return to estimate years to double your money.

- 7% return: 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10 years to double
- 10% return: 72 ÷ 10 ≈ 7 years to double
- 3% return: 72 ÷ 3 ≈ 24 years to double

## The Power of Starting Early

The biggest factor in compounding is time. Consider two investors:

**Investor A**: Starts at 25, invests $5,000/year until 35 (10 years, $50,000 total), then stops but keeps the money invested until 65.

**Investor B**: Starts at 35, invests $5,000/year all the way until 65 (30 years, $150,000 total).

At 8% per year, Investor A ends up with more money than Investor B — despite investing one-third as much — simply because of the extra 10 years of compounding in their 20s.

## Regular Contributions Amplify Compounding

Adding regular contributions (monthly investments, salary sacrifice, automatic transfers) dramatically accelerates wealth accumulation. Each new contribution starts its own compounding journey. This is the mathematical basis for why investing small amounts consistently is so powerful.

## Inflation and Real Returns

Compound growth must be considered in real (inflation-adjusted) terms. If your investment grows at 7% but inflation is 3%, your real return is approximately 4%. The compounding of real returns is what builds genuine purchasing power over time.

## Debt and Negative Compounding

Compound interest works against you on debt. A $30,000 credit card balance at 20% annual interest, if left unpaid, compounds rapidly. This is why paying down high-interest debt is equivalent to a guaranteed high-return investment.
