# Property Investing in Australia

Residential property is one of Australia's most popular investment classes. Australians have historically achieved strong capital growth in major cities, supported by population growth, land constraints, and favourable tax treatment.

## How Property Investment Works

You purchase a property (house or apartment), typically using a mortgage, and rent it out. Returns come from rental income and capital growth over time. Rental yield (annual rent ÷ property value) typically ranges from 2%–6% in Australian capital cities, while long-term capital growth in major cities has averaged approximately 6%–8% per year historically.

## Financing

Most investment properties are purchased with a mortgage. Key loan structures include:

**Principal and interest (P&I)**: Repayments cover both interest and principal. Builds equity and ownership over time.

**Interest-only (IO)**: Repayments cover only interest for an agreed period (typically 1–5 years). Lower repayments, maintaining maximum deductibility but not building equity.

Lenders typically require a 20% deposit for investment properties to avoid lenders mortgage insurance (LMI). Investment loan interest rates are usually 0.2%–0.5% higher than owner-occupier rates.

## Rental Yield vs Capital Growth

- **High yield properties**: Often regional areas, apartments, or lower-value properties. Strong cash flow but lower capital growth prospects.
- **High growth properties**: Inner-city and coastal markets. Capital growth potential but often negatively geared with low yields.

Most investors in Sydney and Melbourne are negatively geared, relying on capital growth to justify the strategy.

## Key Costs

- Stamp duty: 3%–6% of property value depending on state, whether you're a first buyer, and whether it's an investment
- Conveyancing/legal fees: ~$1,000–$3,000
- Building and pest inspection: ~$500–$1,000
- Ongoing: property management (7–10% of rent), rates, insurance, maintenance
- Selling costs: agent commission (1.5%–3%), legal fees

## Property vs Shares

Property offers leverage (typically 80% LVR), tangibility, and rental income, but is illiquid, concentrated, and high-maintenance. Shares (especially index ETFs) offer liquidity, diversification, and low management effort, but are volatile and require discipline during drawdowns. Both asset classes have merit in a diversified portfolio.

## Risks

- Interest rate rises increase mortgage repayments, worsening cash flow
- Vacancy periods (no rental income while mortgage continues)
- Property-specific risks: structural issues, difficult tenants, falling values
- Concentration risk: a large portion of wealth in one asset
- Regulatory risk: changes to negative gearing or CGT rules would affect investment returns

## REITs — Property Without the Management

Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), called A-REITs on the ASX, offer exposure to commercial, retail, and industrial property without direct ownership. They distribute rental income as dividends and trade like shares. Examples include Dexus, Scentre Group, and Goodman Group.
