# Currency Risk and Hedging for Australian Investors

When Australians invest in international assets, they take on currency risk — the risk that changes in exchange rates will affect their returns in Australian dollar terms. Understanding and managing this risk is an important part of global portfolio management.

## What is Currency Risk?

If you invest $10,000 AUD in US shares when the AUD/USD rate is 0.65 (i.e., 1 AUD = 0.65 USD), you are investing US$6,500. If the AUD strengthens to 0.72 when you sell, your US$7,800 return is worth only AUD$10,833 — the AUD appreciation has eroded some of your gains. Conversely, if the AUD weakens to 0.58, the same US$7,800 is worth AUD$13,448 — currency movement amplified your returns.

## The AUD's Behaviour

The Australian dollar is a commodity currency — it tends to strengthen when global growth is strong (higher demand for Australian resources) and weaken during global downturns. This creates a natural hedge: when global sharemarkets fall, the AUD typically falls too, partially offsetting losses for unhedged international investors.

This counter-cyclical behaviour is one reason many financial planners recommend unhedged international equity exposure for long-term Australian investors.

## Hedged vs Unhedged ETFs

Many international ETFs come in both hedged and unhedged versions:
- **VGS** (Vanguard MSCI World ex-AU, unhedged): full currency exposure to USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, etc.
- **VGAD** (Vanguard MSCI World, AUD-hedged): uses currency forwards to neutralise exchange rate movements

Hedged ETFs have slightly higher management costs (0.02%–0.15% additional) to cover the cost of currency forwards.

## When to Hedge

Hedging makes more sense:
- Over shorter time horizons (where currency volatility dominates)
- When the AUD appears historically undervalued (hedging locks in the cheap AUD)
- For fixed income investments where currency swamps return

Hedging is less important for:
- Long-term equity investing (currency effects average out over decades)
- When AUD is historically strong
- When portfolio size doesn't justify the additional complexity

## Direct Currency Exposure

If you buy US shares directly via a broker like Stake or Interactive Brokers, you are converting AUD to USD. You can leave the USD in a USD account and convert back when needed, or convert immediately. Large conversions benefit from using a foreign exchange specialist (Wise, OFX) rather than a bank, as bank spreads can cost 1%–3%.

## Impact of RBA Rate Differentials

Interest rate differentials between countries affect exchange rates through carry trades. When Australian rates are higher than US rates, the AUD tends to attract foreign capital and strengthen. When the Fed raises rates aggressively (as in 2022), the USD strengthens broadly against most currencies including the AUD.
