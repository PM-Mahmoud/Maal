# Central Banks and Their Impact on Markets

Central banks are the most powerful institutional actors in financial markets. Their decisions on interest rates and money supply affect every asset class globally — from government bonds to shares, property, currencies, and commodities.

## The US Federal Reserve

The Federal Reserve (the Fed) is the world's most influential central bank. Its dual mandate is price stability (2% inflation) and maximum employment. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meets 8 times per year to set the federal funds rate. Fed communications — including FOMC statements, minutes, and Jerome Powell's press conferences — are closely scrutinised by global markets.

**Key tools:**
- Federal funds rate (overnight rate target)
- Quantitative Easing (QE): purchasing bonds to inject money into the economy
- Quantitative Tightening (QT): reducing the Fed's balance sheet
- Forward guidance: communicating future policy intentions

## The European Central Bank (ECB)

The ECB sets monetary policy for 20 eurozone countries. Its primary mandate is price stability (2% inflation). The ECB meets every six weeks. The ECB's decisions affect European bond yields and the EUR/AUD exchange rate, indirectly influencing returns for Australian investors in European assets.

## The Bank of Japan (BOJ)

Japan maintained near-zero or negative interest rates for decades as part of an extraordinary effort to fight deflation. In 2024–25, the BOJ began gradually normalising policy as inflation returned to Japan. BOJ policy changes (particularly the yield curve control adjustments) have triggered significant global market volatility, as large amounts of capital had been borrowed cheaply in Japan and invested globally (the "yen carry trade").

## The Bank of England (BOE)

The BOE sets monetary policy for the UK, targeting 2% CPI inflation. Its decisions affect UK asset prices, GBP/AUD exchange rates, and indirectly Australian investors holding FTSE-exposed ETFs.

## The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA)

Australia's central bank targets 2%–3% CPI inflation. The RBA's decisions on the cash rate directly affect Australian variable mortgage rates, term deposit rates, and the AUD exchange rate. Higher Australian rates relative to the US attract foreign capital into AUD, strengthening the currency.

## The Interest Rate Cycle

Central banks move in cycles:
1. **Easing cycle**: Cutting rates to stimulate growth → good for shares, property, and bonds
2. **Tightening cycle**: Raising rates to reduce inflation → typically negative for growth assets, positive for cash and short-term bonds
3. **Neutral/Pause**: Holding rates stable while monitoring data

Markets often price in expected rate changes before they occur. Bond markets in particular reflect the market's expectation of the future path of interest rates.

## Quantitative Easing and Tightening

When interest rates hit zero, central banks turn to QE — purchasing government bonds and mortgage-backed securities to inject liquidity and push down longer-term interest rates. QE expanded central bank balance sheets dramatically from 2008–2022. QT (the reverse) involves not reinvesting maturing bonds, shrinking the balance sheet and tightening financial conditions.

## How Central Bank Policy Affects Asset Classes

| Scenario | Shares | Bonds | Property | Cash | AUD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate cuts | ↑ (positive) | ↑ (price rises) | ↑ (lower mortgage cost) | ↓ (lower yield) | ↓ typically |
| Rate hikes | ↓ (growth stocks most) | ↓ (price falls) | ↓ (higher mortgage cost) | ↑ (higher yield) | ↑ typically |
| QE | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | — | varies |
